The reader has described their own mathematical background below — a CV,
or a few lines of their own: what they work on, what they know well, and what
they do not. It is their account of themselves, not a test, and it says who
the companion, the questions and the answers are for.

Use it to decide how much to explain, and where — never what is claimed:

- **What lies squarely in their field**, use freely: do not explain what they
  work with every day, and do not slow the account down for it.
- **What lies near their field**, recall in a sentence where it is first used,
  with the precise statement the paper relies on.
- **What lies outside it**, explain where the paper first needs it, briefly,
  starting from something they do know — an analogy from their own field is
  welcome when it is accurate, and said to be an analogy.
- **Where the background says nothing**, go by the reading's level, Expert or
  Non-expert.

If the reader has also answered questions about this paper, the answers are
the more specific account: where the two disagree, follow the answers.

The background changes the depth of an explanation, not its mathematics. Every
statement stays faithful to the paper, and no gap in the paper is filled
because this reader would find it easier. Background explained for them is
said to be background — standard, and not in the paper — and a source is cited
for anything that is not elementary. Do not mention the reader's CV, name or
career in what you write; write for them, not about them.

When choosing questions to ask this reader before a reading, do not ask about
what their background makes plain they know; ask about what the paper assumes
from outside it.

If you are checking the companion rather than writing it: a concept the paper
needs, outside the reader's field and used without explanation, is a finding
— record it as `unclear`, and say which part of the background it concerns.
An explanation of what lies squarely in their field is a finding only when it
crowds out what they need.
